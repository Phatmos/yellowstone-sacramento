## The short answer

**A complete exterior proposal should separate the fixed base scope from unresolved selections, optional upgrades and genuinely unknown quantities.** Use an **allowance** for a defined selection that is not final, an **alternate** for optional work that can be accepted or declined, and a **unit price** for measurable work whose final quantity cannot yet be confirmed. Each entry should state what is included, how the final amount will be calculated and what approval is required before the price changes.

This guide narrows the broader questions in our [Sacramento exterior-estimate checklist](/blog/sacramento-exterior-estimate-questions/). It focuses on the pricing tools that often look precise on a proposal but still leave important decisions unresolved.

## Start with a fixed, measurable base scope

Allowances and alternates should not replace a clear description of the work. The base proposal still needs to identify:

- the exact deck, fence run, wall elevations, window openings or sliding patio door included;
- demolition and disposal limits;
- products, profiles, colors and installation systems already selected;
- preparation, flashing, framing, fastening and finish-restoration work;
- protection for landscaping, interiors and materials designated to remain;
- permit, inspection, cleanup and closeout responsibilities;
- explicit exclusions and owner responsibilities.

California's Contractors State License Board says a home-improvement contract should describe in detail the work, products, quantities and significant materials, as well as the price, payment schedule, permits and completion information. A row that says only “allowance as needed” does not supply those details.

Before signing, compare the written proposal with site photographs. The [pre-demolition exterior photo checklist](/blog/sacramento-exterior-project-photos-before-demolition/) shows how to label locations and items that must remain.

## Allowances: a budget for a decision, not a blank check

An allowance is useful when the category is known but the exact selection is not. For example, a homeowner may know that new composite decking is included but still be comparing two product lines or colors. The allowance creates a temporary budget until the specified selection is approved.

A useful allowance states:

| Allowance detail | What the proposal should say |
| --- | --- |
| Category | The exact product, finish or work decision still open |
| Included amount | A dollar amount or quantity, not “TBD” |
| Cost basis | Material only, or material plus tax, delivery, labor and markup |
| Baseline assumption | The product class, dimensions or performance level used for planning |
| Selection deadline | The date needed to protect ordering and schedule |
| Reconciliation | How a selection above or below the allowance changes the contract |
| Approval | Who signs the selection or related change order |

### Example: decking selection allowance

“Composite decking” is too broad. A better allowance identifies the square footage, board family or tier, color category and whether clips, border boards, fascia, tax, delivery and installation are included. Joist spacing and border layout belong in the base design or a documented alternate.

### Example: siding finish allowance

For siding, distinguish cladding from trim, WRB, flashing, fasteners and finish. A paint allowance should identify preparation, number of colors and the boundaries of the painted work.

## Alternates: price a real choice without rewriting the project

An alternate is a complete optional scope with a defined price effect. It lets the owner compare choices while preserving a stable base proposal.

Common exterior alternates may include:

- wood decking versus a named composite product;
- standard railing versus a selected cable or drink-rail system;
- one fence gate versus a wider framed gate;
- one siding elevation versus additional elevations;
- insert window installation versus a full-frame approach where both are technically suitable;
- standard patio-door screen versus a product-specific upgraded screen.

Every alternate should say whether it **adds to**, **deducts from** or **replaces** part of the base price. It should also identify schedule effects, drawing changes and any work that becomes unnecessary when the alternate is selected.

Avoid upgrade prices detached from scope. A railing alternate should name the system, footage, post layout, stair sections, finish, blocking and installation boundaries.

## Unit prices: define both the unit and the trigger

A unit price can be useful when the condition is foreseeable but the final quantity cannot be confirmed until demolition or field measurement. Exterior examples include damaged sheathing revealed behind siding, isolated deck-framing repairs after boards are removed, or additional fence-post excavation under a documented condition.

A complete unit-price line identifies:

1. **The unit:** square foot, linear foot, each, hour or another measurable quantity.
2. **The included work:** removal, material, labor, fasteners, finish, disposal and tax or markup treatment.
3. **The trigger:** the observable condition that makes the work necessary.
4. **The measurement method:** who measures, where it is recorded and how partial units are treated.
5. **The approval point:** when photographs, quantity and price are presented for written authorization.
6. **The limit:** a not-to-exceed quantity or the point where a separate repair proposal is required.

For example, “replace damaged sheathing — $___ per square foot” remains incomplete if it does not say which panel type and thickness are included, whether insulation or framing repairs are excluded, and how openings and small irregular pieces are measured.

Unit pricing should not turn normal installation tasks into extras. Product-required flashing, fasteners, cleanup and preparation belong in the base scope.

## Separate concealed conditions from incomplete estimating

Some conditions cannot be verified without removing the existing assembly. A proposal can define an inspection hold point without pretending the condition is known.

The process should be:

1. Stop at the agreed exposure point.
2. Photograph and label the condition.
3. Compare it with the original scope and stated assumptions.
4. Measure the affected quantity.
5. Apply an agreed unit price or prepare a written change order.
6. Obtain approval before the changed work proceeds.

California CSLB guidance says changes to the price or scope must be documented in a written change order signed before the changed work begins. The change order should describe the changed work, added or deducted cost and effect on progress payments. An allowance reconciliation or unit-price extension should follow the same disciplined written trail.

## Keep a one-page proposal reconciliation

After selections begin, the project total should remain traceable. A useful reconciliation sheet contains:

| Budget component | Original amount | Approved change | Current amount |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Base contract | Stated contract price | — | Current base |
| Accepted alternates | Listed separately | Add or deduct | Updated total |
| Allowances | Original allowance | Selection variance | Reconciled amount |
| Unit-price work | Zero or stated quantity | Approved measured quantity | Extended amount |
| Other change orders | — | Signed additions or deductions | Updated total |

The current total, remaining decisions and next payment milestone should be understandable from one record—not scattered texts or invoices.

## A final proposal review before signing

Use this sequence:

- Verify the project address and every included work area.
- Read the base scope without counting any alternate as included.
- Confirm each allowance amount, cost basis and selection deadline.
- Mark accepted and declined alternates.
- Review every unit price, measurement method and approval limit.
- Confirm permits, inspections, schedule assumptions and payment milestones.
- Attach dated drawings, photos, product schedules and clarifications referenced by the proposal.
- Confirm that verbal promises appear in the written agreement.
- Keep a completely filled-in copy signed by the parties.

This is practical scope guidance, not legal advice. Review current CSLB consumer information and consult a qualified California professional when contract language or project risk warrants it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an allowance the same as a fixed price?

No. An allowance is a temporary budget for a defined unresolved selection or quantity. The proposal should explain how the final choice is reconciled and which costs are included in the allowance.

### Should an alternate be included in the contract total?

Only if it is clearly accepted. The document should mark each alternate as accepted or declined and show whether it adds to, deducts from or replaces a base-scope amount.

### Can a contractor use a unit price without a change order?

The contract can state the agreed unit price, but the additional quantity and resulting price change should still be documented and approved in writing before the changed work proceeds. Follow the current contract and California requirements for the specific project.

### What happens if concealed damage exceeds the unit-price limit?

The proposal should define the limit or review point. Once the condition exceeds that boundary, stop, document the finding and prepare a separate written repair scope or change order rather than extending an open-ended quantity.
