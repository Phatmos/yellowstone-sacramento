## The useful answer: fence cost is a scope, not one price per foot

Homeowners often begin with one question: **How much does a fence cost per linear foot in Sacramento?** Linear footage matters, but a per-foot number by itself can hide the decisions that make two fences completely different projects.

A 60-foot straight run on level ground with one pedestrian gate is not comparable to a 240-foot replacement with demolition, slopes, three corners, a double gate and limited access. A responsible estimate should explain those differences instead of hiding them inside one unexplained number.

The basic planning formula is:

> **Fence project cost = layout and footage + material system + posts and foundations + gates + site conditions + removal + finish and approvals**

Here are the nine factors that usually move the total the most.

## 1. Total linear footage and number of transitions

Longer fences use more boards, rails, posts, concrete and labor. Corners, steps, short returns and changes in direction also add terminal posts and layout work. Ask the estimator to separate the measured footage from the number of corners and gate openings.

## 2. Fence material

Common Sacramento-area choices include:

- Pressure-treated wood
- Redwood
- Cedar
- Vinyl
- Composite
- Ornamental aluminum
- Chain-link

The material affects more than the visible panel. Each system may require different posts, rails, fasteners, caps and gate reinforcement. Compare complete systems—not the price of loose boards at a store.

## 3. Height and privacy style

A taller fence normally needs more material and may need a stronger post schedule. Board-on-board privacy uses more boards than a simple side-by-side layout. Horizontal fencing can need closer posts or intermediate support to control long runs. Decorative caps, lattice, trim and framed panels also add material and detail work.

## 4. Posts and foundation conditions

Posts are one of the most important parts of the fence and one of the easiest places for a cheap estimate to cut scope. The proposal should identify:

- Wood, steel, vinyl or system-specific posts
- Approximate post spacing
- Concrete and excavation assumptions
- Gate-post reinforcement
- Treatment of existing post holes or old concrete

Rocky soil, buried concrete, roots or poor access can change the installation effort after work begins. Ask how concealed conditions will be handled.

## 5. Gates and hardware

Pedestrian gates, double gates and rolling commercial gates should be priced separately. A wide opening requires more than two hinges and a latch: gate weight, diagonal bracing, post reinforcement, clearance and how the gate meets sloped ground all matter.

## 6. Demolition and disposal

Replacing an old fence can include board removal, posts, concrete, vines and disposal fees. A quote that says “remove existing fence” should clarify whether buried concrete is included and whether landscaping attached to the fence must be cleared before arrival.

## 7. Grade, access and working space

Level soil with direct driveway access is faster to work on than a steep side yard reached through a narrow gate. Ask the estimator to note:

- Slope and stepped sections
- Distance from parking to the work area
- Width of access paths
- Irrigation, trees and retaining walls
- Neighbor-side access restrictions

These facts are more useful than a generic “difficult site” charge.

## 8. Stain, sealer, paint or color upgrades

Wood can be left to weather or finished with a compatible exterior product. Vinyl, composite and metal systems may have premium colors or textures. Confirm whether finishing is included, when it can be applied and who is responsible for future recoating.

## 9. Property, HOA and local requirements

The [City of Sacramento permit guidance](https://www.cityofsacramento.gov/community-development/building/permit-services/required-permits.html) currently notes that some fences not over seven feet may be exempt from a building permit, but it also states that fencing remains subject to Planning Division approval and applicable standards. Corner visibility, front-yard location, easements, historic districts, pool barriers and HOA rules can create additional requirements.

Do not assume that “no building permit” means “no rules.” Verify the project address and final layout before ordering materials.

## What a complete fence estimate should show

Use this checklist when comparing proposals:

1. Measured or estimated linear footage
2. Material, profile and grade
3. Fence height and style
4. Post type and approximate spacing
5. Gate count, width and hardware
6. Demolition and disposal scope
7. Finish, cap and trim details
8. Known site assumptions and exclusions
9. Permit, HOA and property-line responsibilities
10. Workmanship warranty and final cleanup

If two estimates are far apart, compare these lines before assuming one contractor simply charges more.

## How to get a reliable number for your property

Send the address, approximate footage, a few photos, preferred material, desired height and gate needs. Then schedule an on-site measurement before treating any budget number as final.
