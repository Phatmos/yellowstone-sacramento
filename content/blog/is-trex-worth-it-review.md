<h2>Is Trex Worth It? Honest Review After 10+ Years</h2>
<p>For many homeowners, <strong>Trex decking</strong> is synonymous with composite — but does it live up to its reputation? After over a decade of installing and servicing decks featuring Trex boards in climates like California, we’re breaking down the real strengths, weaknesses, and long-term value.</p>
<hr>
<h2>What Is Trex Decking?</h2>
<p>Trex is one of the largest and most widely recognized manufacturers of <strong>composite decking</strong>, known for high-performance capped boards made from recycled wood fibers and plastic polymers.</p>
<p>Unlike traditional wood, Trex boards are engineered to resist rot, insects, and moisture — and they come with long-term warranties (often 25–50 years).</p>
<p>But real performance goes beyond manufacturer promises.</p>

<hr>
<h2>Performance After 10+ Years</h2>
<h3><strong>1. Rot &#x26; Moisture Resistance</strong></h3>
<p>Trex performs well in Northern California because capped boards resist seasonal moisture and require less upkeep than exposed wood under strong UV conditions.</p>
<p>Because Trex boards:</p>
<ul>
<li>Don’t absorb moisture</li>
<li>Resist fungal decay</li>
<li>Don’t warp like wood</li>
</ul>
<p>They hold up significantly better than untreated lumber.</p>
<h3><strong>2. Insect &#x26; Pest Resistance</strong></h3>
<p>Unlike cedar or pressure-treated wood, Trex won’t attract termites or wood-boring insects — a key advantage in humid environments.</p>
<h3><strong>3. Structural Stability</strong></h3>
<p>Properly installed Trex decks don’t cup, twist, or split over time — issues common with lumber in regions with large seasonal temperature swings.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Appearance: How Trex Ages</h2>
<p>Trex boards are designed to:</p>
<ul>
<li>Resist fading</li>
<li>Maintain color longer than wood</li>
<li>Withstand surface wear</li>
</ul>
<p>That said, over the course of a decade you may notice:</p>
<ul>
<li>Light color fading in direct sunlight</li>
<li>Minor surface scratching from furniture or foot traffic</li>
</ul>
<p>But these cosmetic changes <strong>don’t impact structural integrity</strong> and are often minimal on premium capped boards.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Maintenance: What to Expect</h2>
<p>One of Trex’s biggest selling points is <em>low maintenance</em> — but it’s not zero maintenance.</p>
<p>Recommended routine care:</p>
<ul>
<li>Annual washing with mild soap and water</li>
<li>Remove debris between boards</li>
<li>Avoid metal shovels or sharp tools</li>
<li>Power-wash carefully if needed</li>
</ul>
<p>Compared to wood (which requires regular staining and sealing), Trex is significantly easier to care for.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Cost vs. Value: Is Trex Worth the Price?</h2>
<p>Trex typically costs more upfront than pressure-treated lumber — roughly <strong>$8–$14 per square foot</strong> (materials only), but the long-term savings add up.</p>
<p><strong>Trex Pros:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Lower maintenance costs</li>
<li>Longer lifespan</li>
<li>Better moisture &#x26; pest resistance</li>
<li>Strong resale value</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Lumber Cons:</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Frequent sealing</li>
<li>Higher repair costs</li>
<li>Shorter lifespan</li>
<li>Susceptible to rot &#x26; insects</li>
</ul>
<p>Over 20+ years, composite often costs <strong>less per year of service</strong> than wood when maintenance and repairs are considered.</p>
<hr>
<h2>Trex vs. Other Composite Brands</h2>

<p>Comparative factors:</p>
<ul>

<li><strong>Color options</strong> — some brands offer wider palettes</li>

<p>Even the best products have trade-offs.</p>

<p>For many homeowners, these are acceptable trade-offs for long-term performance.</p>

<h2>Final Verdict: Is Trex Worth It?</h2>
<p>In most cases — <strong>yes</strong>.</p>
<p>If your priorities are:
