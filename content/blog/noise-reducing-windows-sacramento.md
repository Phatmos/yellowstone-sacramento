## Identify the noise before shopping for glass

Write down what you hear, when it occurs and where it enters. Low-frequency truck or aircraft noise behaves differently from voices or a barking dog. Stand near the glass, sash, wall vents and door gaps; a high-performance window cannot correct sound entering through another weak part of the room.

## Ask for OITC and STC—then compare exact configurations

STC is commonly used for speech-range sound, while OITC gives more weight to lower-frequency outdoor noise such as transportation. Manufacturers including [Andersen](https://www.andersenwindows.com/for-professionals/pro-views/sound-abatement) explain that higher ratings indicate better laboratory performance. Compare ratings for the exact size, operating style and glass package quoted, not a brand-wide claim.

### Glass thickness and laminated glass

Different pane thicknesses can help address overlapping resonances, and laminated glass can improve acoustic performance. More panes alone do not guarantee the best result. Ask for tested ratings instead of relying on “soundproof” language.

### Air space and frame design

The spacing between panes, sash seals and frame construction influence performance. A fixed window will often test differently from an operating unit. Balance sound goals with ventilation, egress and cleaning needs.

### Installation gaps

Sound follows air leaks. The perimeter between the new frame and wall must be insulated and sealed as part of a complete installation. Existing wall vents, recessed boxes or poorly sealed doors may remain the dominant path.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are noise-reducing windows soundproof?

No. They can reduce transmitted sound, but results depend on the complete window, installation and other paths through the room.

### Should every window receive the same glass package?

Not necessarily. Prioritize the elevations and rooms exposed to the source, while keeping the exterior appearance and code requirements coordinated.
